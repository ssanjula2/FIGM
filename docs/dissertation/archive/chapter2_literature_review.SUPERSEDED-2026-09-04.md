# Chapter 2: Literature Review

*Scope note: this review is built exclusively on the works already listed in `references.md`. It does not introduce new sources. Where a specific paper's own detailed findings are not directly load-bearing for a claim made here, this chapter describes its topic and relevance rather than asserting specific results from it that cannot be independently verified against this repository's own evidence.*

## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter situates the Framework-Independent Guideline Model (FIGM) against four bodies of prior work: software quality and accessibility standards (Section 2.2), the design science research paradigm this dissertation adopts as its methodology (Section 2.3), the compiler and program-analysis theory underlying FIGM's detection engine (Section 2.4), software design patterns and the TypeScript type system underlying its implementation (Section 2.5), empirical software engineering research methods underlying its evaluation approach (Section 2.6), and the most directly comparable prior empirical work on static analysis tooling for accessibility (Section 2.7). Section 2.8 synthesizes these threads into the specific research gap this dissertation addresses.

## 2.2 Software Quality Models and Accessibility Standards

FIGM's Domain Layer (Chapter 3, Section 3.2.1) partitions quality concerns into Performance Efficiency and Accessibility. This partition is not an arbitrary design choice; it is grounded directly in two standards. ISO/IEC 25010:2011, *Systems and software Quality Requirements and Evaluation (SQuaRE) — System and software quality models* (International Organization for Standardization, 2011), defines Performance Efficiency as one of several product quality characteristics applicable independently of implementation technology, giving FIGM's performance rules (the `FIGM-PERF-*` series) a standards-based, rather than ad hoc, justification for what counts as a performance concern in the first place. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 (World Wide Web Consortium, 2023) play the equivalent role for FIGM's accessibility rules (`FIGM-A11Y-*`): every accessibility rule in the canonical specification (`figm-spec.json`) carries a `standardTraceability` field naming a specific WCAG 2.2 success criterion, so that a given rule's justification is traceable to a named, externally-defined normative requirement rather than to the tool's own unstated judgment of what accessibility means.

The relevance of both standards to this dissertation is specifically as a *source of formal vocabulary* for the Domain and Concern layers of FIGM's meta-model — not as a specification of *how* to detect a violation of either standard in source code. That translation step, from a standard's natural-language success criterion to an executable AST-level check, is FIGM's own contribution and is not addressed by either standard itself.

## 2.3 Design Science Research Methodology

This dissertation follows the Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm as formulated by Hevner et al. (2004), which frames the construction of an innovative software artifact — here, FIGM itself — as a legitimate mode of Information Systems research contribution, evaluated by whether the artifact demonstrably addresses a real, previously unsolved (or incompletely solved) problem, rather than solely by novelty of theory. Chapter 3 (Section 3.1) adopts this framing explicitly as the dissertation's overall methodological stance.

Peffers et al. (2007) operationalize Hevner et al.'s DSR paradigm into a concrete, six-activity process model — problem identification and motivation, definition of objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication — intended to give DSR researchers a repeatable, checkable process rather than only a philosophical justification. This dissertation's own structure mirrors that sequence directly: the problem and objectives are established in this chapter and Chapter 1, the design and development of the artifact is reported in Chapters 3 and 4, demonstration and evaluation are reported in Chapter 5, and Chapter 6 constitutes the communication of results, including the honest reporting of what has not yet been evaluated (RQ1's FCP/TTI experiment and RQ4's expert-review comparison — Chapter 1, Section 1.4 — plus the separate, supplementary developer-usability evaluation, Chapter 6 Section 6.2.1/6.6) rather than only what has.

A methodological commitment that follows directly from adopting Peffers et al.'s process is that the *evaluation* activity must be as rigorously reported as the *design* activity. This dissertation treats that commitment as binding: Chapter 5's benchmark circularity disclosure, Chapter 6's honest 0-of-20 usability-participant accounting, and its honest 0-real-expert-submission accounting for RQ4, are direct consequences of taking the DSR evaluation activity seriously rather than as a pro forma section.

## 2.4 Compiler Theory and Program Analysis Foundations

FIGM's real-AST detection layer (Chapter 4, Section 4.2) is built directly on classical compiler construction theory. Aho et al.'s *Compilers: Principles, Techniques, and Tools* (2006) — the standard reference for lexical analysis, parsing, and abstract syntax tree construction — describes the general architecture FIGM's own parsing layer instantiates: source text is lexed and parsed into a tree structure (in FIGM's case, via the TypeScript compiler API, `@vue/compiler-dom`, or `angular-html-parser`, depending on the target language), and analysis is then performed over that tree rather than over raw text. FIGM's own documented migration away from a text/regex-heuristic detection layer and toward this AST-based architecture (Chapter 4, Section 4.2; `docs/architecture/AST_MIGRATION_MAP.md`) is, in these terms, a migration from ad hoc pattern matching toward the standard compiler-theoretic approach to program representation.

Nielson et al.'s *Principles of Program Analysis* (2005) surveys the broader family of static analysis techniques — including data-flow analysis and abstract-interpretation-based analyses — that operate *over* such a tree or control-flow representation to answer questions a syntactic pattern match cannot: not merely "does this syntactic shape appear," but "what values can this variable actually take at this program point." This distinction is directly relevant to honestly scoping FIGM's own detection rules. Several of FIGM's rules — most notably `FIGM-PERF-01` ("Invariant Object Allocation") and `FIGM-REACT-03` ("Avoid Direct React State Mutation") — have a documented, explicit `knownLimitation`: they identify a syntactic *shape* (an object literal in a loop scope; a mutation-method call on a name with file-wide evidence of being component state) without performing the full data-flow or binding analysis that Nielson et al. describe as necessary to answer the underlying question with certainty. `FIGM-PERF-01`'s detector was narrowed during this dissertation's own development (see Chapter 4) to exclude the specific, verifiable case of an object literal that provably references its enclosing loop's own bound variable — a bounded, single-hop check, explicitly *not* the general data-flow analysis Nielson et al. describe, and documented as such rather than presented as a complete solution to the harder problem.

Cousot and Cousot's foundational 1977 paper on abstract interpretation established the theoretical framework within which *sound* (no false negatives, by construction) static analysis is defined, via approximation of a program's concrete semantics by a computable abstract one. FIGM's detection engine does not claim soundness in this formal sense; its own rule specifications self-disclose specific, concrete false-positive and false-negative classes (Chapter 4; each rule's `knownLimitation` field) rather than claiming the soundness guarantee abstract interpretation theory defines. Citing Cousot and Cousot here is therefore not a claim that FIGM implements abstract interpretation, but an acknowledgment of the theoretical ceiling against which FIGM's own, more limited, syntactic-and-shape-based approach should be understood — the gap between what FIGM's rules can prove and what a fully sound analysis would require is exactly the gap this theory defines.

## 2.5 Software Design and Type-System Foundations

FIGM's implementation makes direct, repeated use of classical object-oriented design patterns as catalogued by Gamma et al. (1994). The `AIProviderSelector` module (Chapter 4), which resolves which of several interchangeable AI backends (Ollama, Claude, or Gemini) is active from environment configuration without any caller needing to know which one, is a direct application of the Strategy pattern. The `RepositoryFactory`/storage-provider architecture, which selects between a JSON-file-backed and a Supabase-backed persistence implementation behind one common interface, is an application of the Factory and Adapter patterns respectively. The framework-specific real-AST builders (`src/ast/vue-template-ast-builder.ts`, `src/ast/angular-template-ast-builder.ts`), which each expose a uniform `ASTNode[]` output from a structurally different underlying parser (`@vue/compiler-dom` versus `angular-html-parser`), are Adapters in the same sense Gamma et al. define the pattern. These are not claimed as novel contributions of this dissertation — they are the direct, intentional application of established, decades-old design vocabulary to keep the system's provider- and framework-specific variability isolated behind stable interfaces.

Bierman et al. (2014) describe the design of TypeScript's structural type system. Their relevance here is narrower and more specific than the compiler-theory works above: FIGM's real-AST detection layer is built directly on the TypeScript compiler's own parser (`ts.createSourceFile`), used specifically because it is a mature, structurally-typed, industrial-strength parser already capable of tolerant (error-recovering) parsing of malformed input — a property FIGM's own resilience contract depends on throughout (Chapter 4). This dissertation does not otherwise depend on or evaluate TypeScript's type-checking behavior itself; the compiler is used here purely as a syntax parser.

General principles of asynchronous and concurrent program design, of the kind surveyed for the .NET/C# ecosystem by Cleary (2019), inform — at the level of general pattern, not language-specific API — FIGM's own design decision to run its dynamic code-execution sandbox (`DynamicProfiler`) as a genuinely separate, non-blocking child process (`spawn`, not `spawnSync`, on the live web server's request path; Chapter 4) specifically so that one slow or hanging analysis cannot block the Node.js event loop for concurrent, unrelated requests. The citation here is to the general async/non-blocking design principle Cleary's work documents for its own ecosystem, not to any C#-specific API FIGM does not use.

## 2.6 Empirical Software Engineering Research Methods

Wohlin et al.'s *Experimentation in Software Engineering* (2012) provides the methodological vocabulary this dissertation uses to structure and report its own empirical evaluation: the distinction between internal, external, and construct validity that organizes Chapter 6's "Threats to Validity" section directly follows this tradition. Of particular relevance to this dissertation's own evaluation practice is Wohlin et al.'s treatment of experimental control and the provenance of evaluation data — a concern this dissertation applies specifically to the question of *who authored the ground truth an evaluation is scored against* (Chapter 5, Section 5.1.1's circularity disclosure), since a detector and its own evaluation oracle sharing a single author is a threat to the independence of a validity claim in exactly the sense this literature treats as a first-order concern, not an afterthought.

## 2.7 Related Empirical Work

Saurabh and Roy (2022) undertook an empirical evaluation of static code analysis tools for web accessibility compliance — the most directly comparable prior empirical work to this dissertation's own accessibility-detection evaluation (Chapter 5). Their study establishes that empirically evaluating accessibility-focused static analysis tooling, rather than assessing it only on qualitative capability grounds, is itself an established line of inquiry that this dissertation's Chapter 5 evaluation continues in the same spirit. This dissertation does not reproduce or extend their specific reported findings — its own contribution is the FIGM artifact and its own, separately-conducted evaluation (Chapter 5), which, as disclosed there, differs from an ideal independent evaluation of the kind their line of work exemplifies in one material respect: FIGM's primary benchmark corpus (`benchmarks/testbed`, `benchmarks/ground-truth.json`) is authored by the same party that built the detector under test, whereas an evaluation against an externally-sourced or independently-annotated corpus would more closely match that standard. Independent annotation of FIGM's own output is a tracked, currently pending activity of this project (`research/independent-annotation/`), not yet complete at the time of this writing.

## 2.8 Synthesis and Research Gap

Taken together, this literature establishes three things this dissertation relies on directly: first, that formal, standards-traceable quality models (Section 2.2) provide a legitimate, non-arbitrary vocabulary for defining what a "guideline" is in the first place; second, that Design Science Research (Section 2.3) provides an accepted methodological basis for treating the construction and evaluation of a software artifact like FIGM as a research contribution in its own right, provided the evaluation activity is conducted and reported with the same rigor as the design activity; and third, that the distinction between syntactic pattern-matching and true data-flow/abstract-interpretation-based analysis (Section 2.4) is well-established prior art that this dissertation uses to honestly bound its own claims, rather than to overstate them.

The specific gap this dissertation addresses, as introduced in Chapter 1, is that no widely-adopted tool combines (a) a single, formally-specified guideline definition projected via real, compiler-derived AST analysis onto multiple distinct frontend frameworks, with (b) a remediation pipeline that verifies — by genuine re-compilation and re-scan, not by inspection of a generated patch alone — that a proposed fix actually resolves the violation it targets. Chapter 5's qualitative comparison against Lighthouse, ESLint, SonarQube, `eslint-plugin-react`, and `angular-eslint` situates FIGM against this gap directly; Chapter 3 presents the formal model and methodology constructed to address it.
